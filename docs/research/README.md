## 1. The problem: the in-basket

| Finding | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Patient-written portal messages rose 153% from 2020 to 2025 (0.99 → 2.50 per active patient per year), across about 2,067 hospitals and 47,100 clinics in Epic Cosmos; office visits rose only from 2.37 to 2.77 per patient, phone calls fell 6% | Long et al., JAMA, 2026-06-22, via [AJMC 2026](https://www.ajmc.com/view/patient-portal-messages-outpace-office-visits) and [Healthcare Dive 2026](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/patient-portal-messages-clinicians-skyrockets-jama/823785/) | M |
| Early in the pandemic, in-basket messages from patients reached 157% of the pre-pandemic average (366 health systems, Dec 2019–Dec 2020) | Holmgren et al., JAMIA 2021, cited in [PMC12686222, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12686222/) | M |
| Primary care physicians spent about 52 minutes per workday on the inbox, 19 of them (37%) outside work hours; patient messages (28%) and results (29%) took the most time (1,275 PCPs) | Akbar et al., JAMIA 2021, via [Kaiser Permanente Division of Research](https://divisionofresearch.kaiserpermanente.org/publications/physicians-electronic-inbox-work-patterns-and-factors-associated-with-high-inbox-work-duration/) | M |
| Physicians received 243 in-basket messages a week on average (30 from patients); above-average system-generated messages were linked to 40% higher odds of burnout | Tai-Seale et al., Health Affairs 2019, via [Sutter Health](https://www.sutterhealth.org/research/publications/md-wellbeing-in-basket-messages-from-ehr-algorithms) | M |
| Message types vary by setting: VA primary care, refills 47.2%, scheduling 17.6%, medication issues 12.9%, health issues 12.7%; ophthalmology, scheduling 29.2%, refills 9.5%, nearly half administrative | [PMC7651909](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7651909), [JMIR Med Inform 2026](https://medinform.jmir.org/2026/1/e96026/PDF) | M |
| e-Visit codes since 2020: CPT 99421 (5–10 min), 99422 (11–20), 99423 (21+), cumulative over 7 days, established patient, patient-initiated; not for scheduling, results or other non-evaluative messages; clinical staff time does not count | [AMA 2020](https://ama-assn.org/practice-management/cpt/new-2020-cpt-codes-recognize-em-work-happens-online), [AAPC](https://aapc.com/codes/cpt-codes-range/99421-99423) | M |
| At UCSF, after billing for messages began (Nov 2021), billing rose about fivefold but under 2% of message threads were billed; threads fell about 15%, all messages about 3% | Holmgren et al., JAMA 2023, via [Tradeoffs 2023](https://tradeoffs.org/2023/03/07/patients-provider-electronic-messaging-fees/) | M |

**What this means for the design (recommendation):** the volume problem is real and has not gone back down, and patient messages are one of the two largest parts of inbox time. But a large share of messages are administrative (refills, scheduling), where a draft adds little and a template or routing works. The demo should handle a few clear message types well, tag each with its type, and leave billing out: e-visit billing depends on clinician time and medical decision-making, which the tool must not guess.

## 2. Evidence on AI-drafted replies

| Finding | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Stanford, 5 weeks, 162 primary care and GI clinicians: drafts used 20% of the time on average; no change in read, write or reply time; task load (61.3 → 47.3) and work exhaustion (1.95 → 1.62) fell; tone and length got the most complaints; no safety signal | Garcia et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024;7(3):e243201, via [Stanford Medicine 2024](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2024/03/ai-patient-messages.html) and [PMC10955355](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10955355/) | H |
| UC San Diego, randomised waiting-list study, 52 primary care physicians: read time rose (median 26 → 31 s), reply time did not change, replies got longer | Tai-Seale et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024;7(4):e246565, [PubMed 38619840](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38619840/) | H |
| Columbia, 983,154 first replies, 1,542 clinicians and staff (2023–2025): drafts used on about 5% of messages; drafted replies went out 29.5% faster than the same clinicians' undrafted ones, but clinicians overall replied 8.6% slower after starting; after-hours work did not fall | JAMA Netw Open 2026, via [Epocrates](https://www.epocrates.com/online/article/clinician-portal-replies-slowed-after-ai-rollout) | M |
| NYU: about 12% of providers used drafts at first, rising to about 20%; drafts cut response time about 7% (about 20 s), much of it lost to review and editing | [NYU Tandon 2025](https://engineering.nyu.edu/news/new-research-reveals-uptake-ai-powered-messaging-healthcare-settings), [TechTarget](https://techtarget.com/patientengagement/news/366633175/AI-drafted-patient-portal-messages-save-7-on-response-times) | M |
| A 16-week multi-specialty cohort: 58% adoption; total response time 157 s with a blank reply vs 153 s with a draft (no difference) | [Frontiers in Digital Health 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2025.1588143/pdf) | M |
| Mass General Brigham, 100 made-up cancer patient questions, 6 radiation oncologists: GPT-4 drafts acceptable unedited 58% of the time; **7.7% of unedited drafts could cause severe harm** (most often by not telling the patient to seek urgent care); drafts were longer, less readable, more education and fewer clinical actions; doctors took 31% of drafts for human writing | Chen et al., Lancet Digital Health 2024, [arXiv 2310.17703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17703), [MGB news](https://news.massgeneralbrigham.org/en/pitfalls-and-opportunities-for-generative-ai-in-patient-messaging-systems) | H |
| 20 primary care physicians reviewing 18 messages, 4 with planted errors: each error was missed by 13–15 of 20 (at least 65%); 35–45% sent the bad draft unedited; only 1 physician caught all 4; 75% still thought drafts safe (automation complacency) | Biro et al., npj Digital Medicine 2025, via [PC Med Project 2025](https://pcmedproject.com/evidence-matters/do-physicians-correctly-identify-errors-in-ai-generated-responses-to-patient-portal-messages/) | M |
| NYU, 344 blinded pairs rated by 16 PCPs: no difference in accuracy, completeness or relevance; AI drafts rated more empathetic but less readable (higher reading level) | Small et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024;7(7):e2422399, [PMC11252893](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11252893) | H |
| Dartmouth, 146,000 primary care messages: drafts often added errors and extra detail and missed follow-up questions; predicted editing could exceed writing from scratch; tuning to a physician's style cut predicted editing 26% (modelled, not measured time) | [Dartmouth 2026](https://home.dartmouth.edu/news/2026/07/ai-mistakes-can-cost-doctors-time-when-writing-patients) (ACL 2026 paper) | M |
| Patients (Duke, 1,455 respondents, vignettes): preferred AI-drafted replies slightly (+0.30 on 5 points); an AI disclosure lowered satisfaction slightly (−0.09 to −0.13); over 75% satisfied either way; authors say keep disclosure | Cavalier et al., JAMA Netw Open 2025;8(3):e250449, via [Medical Economics 2025](https://www.medicaleconomics.com/view/patients-prefer-ai-portal-messages-until-they-learn-the-source) | M |
| Patients accept AI-drafted messages mainly on condition that a clinician reviews them before sending | [telehealth.org 2025](https://telehealth.org/news/patients-accept-ai-drafted-portal-messages-but-only-with-clinician-review/) | L |
| Epic's In Basket draft feature: about 150 health systems, nearly 15,000 clinicians (2024), "1 million drafts a month" — **vendor claim**; one site reports 40,000–50,000 drafts a month | [eMarketer 2024](https://www.emarketer.com/content/physicians-using-genai-respond-their-patients--mychart-messages), [Fierce Healthcare 2024](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/ai-and-machine-learning/himss24-how-epic-building-out-ai-ambient-technology-clinicians), [WashU IT 2026](https://it.wustl.edu/2026/04/ai-at-the-point-of-care-how-epic-enabled-tools-are-reshaping-clinical-workflows/) | M |

**What this means for the design (recommendation):** the evidence agrees on four things: drafts do not reliably save time; they may lower felt burden; they are longer and harder to read than clinicians' replies; and reviewers miss planted errors most of the time. So the honest claim for the demo is not "saves time" but "every draft is checked in code before a clinician sees it, and the checks are measured". The code must catch the failures humans miss (a wrong dose, a missed "go to the ER"), keep drafts short and at a plain reading level, and record whether the clinician sent, edited or discarded each draft so use and edit rates can be shown.

**What this means for the design (recommendation):** a clinician reading and approving every reply is what keeps AB 3030's disclaimer optional, meets Texas SB 1188's review rule, and keeps the tool on the clinician-facing side of FDA's line; so approval must be enforced in code and bound to the exact text. Still disclose AI help in each message (Duke patients' small dip in satisfaction is a fair price, and Texas HB 149 asks for it). Never send anything to the patient without approval: no auto-replies, no patient-facing chat. The model provider must be one that signs a BAA in any real use; the demo states this rather than claiming it.

| A published list of what drafting systems check before a clinician sees a draft (dose changes, diagnoses, promises): **not found**; studies rely on the clinician to catch it | n/a | n/a |

**What this means for the design (recommendation):** red flags are checked in code before any model call, from a fixed, tested word and phrase list (chest pain, can't breathe, suicidal thoughts, overdose, stroke signs and the like), with the model's urgency label only able to raise priority, never lower it. A flagged message gets no AI draft; it goes to the top of the queue with a fixed "call 911 / 988 / the clinic now" template the clinician can send. Every draft is checked in code for: medication names or doses not in the record, any changed dose, diagnosis words, promises ("will", "guarantee", dates and times not in the data), numbers that do not match the record, length and reading level. A failed check blocks the draft. The queue shows each message's age against a stated reply target (e.g. 2 business days).

## Open decisions

1. **Scope:** the first version drafts replies to patient portal messages for one made-up primary care clinic, inside the clinic's own queue; no patient-facing chat, no auto-replies, no other channels. **Recommended answer:** yes.
2. **Message types:** handle four types — medication questions and refill requests, symptom questions, test-result questions, and scheduling/admin — with each message tagged by type, and scheduling/admin answered from fixed templates rather than the model. **Recommended answer:** yes.
3. **Record data the model may see:** only the message text plus the patient's active conditions, current medications (name, dose, frequency), allergies, the last 12 months of observations and upcoming appointments, with no name, date of birth, address, record number or contact details (the code fills in names after drafting). **Recommended answer:** yes.
4. **Clinic guidance:** the model also gets the clinic's own short written guidance (reply style, refill policy, when to book a visit), stored as versioned text the clinic edits, and each draft records which version it used. **Recommended answer:** yes.
5. **Red flags and triage:** a fixed, tested red-flag list runs in code before any model call; a flagged message gets no AI draft, jumps to the top of the queue and offers a fixed "call 911 / 988 / the clinic now" template; the model's urgency label can raise priority but never lower it. **Recommended answer:** yes.
6. **What a draft must never do (checked in code, blocking):** name a diagnosis, start, stop or change a medication or dose, mention a medicine or number not in the record, promise a date, time or outcome, or exceed a set length and reading level (about grade 8). **Recommended answer:** yes.
7. **Approval:** a clinician (not staff) edits and approves the exact text, the approval is bound to its content hash, any edit after approval needs a new approval, and checks run again on the edited text. **Recommended answer:** yes.
8. **Disclosure wording:** every sent reply ends with "This reply was drafted with the help of AI and reviewed and approved by [clinician name]. To reach a person, call [clinic phone]." **Recommended answer:** yes.
9. **Channel and data format:** replies go back into the made-up portal (messages stored as FHIR `Communication`, linked by `inResponseTo`, served from a pinned local HAPI server); no email or SMS in the first version. **Recommended answer:** yes.
10. **Measurement:** a labelled test set of about 80 made-up messages; report red-flag recall (target 100% on the set), check pass and fail rates, the share of drafts sent unedited, edited or discarded, and approval time; no claim that the tool saves time. **Recommended answer:** yes.
11. **Model:** Claude Sonnet through the owner's API key (`OWNER_ANTHROPIC_API_KEY`), with the README stating that any real use needs a provider that signs a BAA. **Recommended answer:** yes.
